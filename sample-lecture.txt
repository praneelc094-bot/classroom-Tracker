## PN junction formation
0:00 Good morning everyone. So today we will start with the PN junction, which is the building block of almost every semiconductor device you will study. When we take a p-type region and an n-type region and bring them together, what happens at the interface? The p side has a lot of holes, the n side has a lot of electrons. Because of this concentration gradient, electrons diffuse from the n side into the p side, and holes diffuse from the p side into the n side.

## Depletion region and built-in potential
0:45 Now when an electron leaves the n side, it leaves behind a positively charged donor ion, which is fixed in the lattice. Similarly, holes leaving the p side leave behind negative acceptor ions. So near the junction we get a region with no free carriers, only fixed charges. This is called the depletion region, or the space charge region. These fixed charges create an electric field pointing from the n side to the p side, and this field opposes further diffusion. At equilibrium, the drift current due to this field exactly balances the diffusion current. The potential difference across the depletion region is called the built-in potential, and for silicon it is typically around 0.6 to 0.7 volts depending on the doping.

## Forward and reverse bias
2:15 So what happens when we apply an external voltage? If we connect the positive terminal to the p side, that is forward bias. The applied voltage opposes the built-in potential, so the barrier height reduces, the depletion region becomes narrower, and diffusion current increases exponentially. That is why the diode current in forward bias follows the exponential relationship with voltage. In reverse bias, we connect the positive terminal to the n side. Now the barrier increases, the depletion width increases, and only a very small reverse saturation current flows, due to minority carriers.

## Zener and avalanche breakdown
3:20 But if we keep increasing the reverse voltage, at some point the current suddenly increases very sharply. This is called breakdown. There are two mechanisms. In heavily doped junctions, the depletion region is very thin and the electric field is extremely high, so electrons can directly tunnel from the valence band of the p side to the conduction band of the n side. This is Zener breakdown, and it is actually quantum mechanical band to band tunnelling. By the way, this same band to band tunnelling is the principle behind the tunnel FET, the TFET, which some of you may work on in your final year projects. In lightly doped junctions, the depletion region is wider, and carriers gain enough energy from the field to knock out more electron hole pairs by impact ionisation. This multiplication is avalanche breakdown.

## Junction capacitance
4:40 Now one more important point. Because the depletion region has charge on both sides and changes with applied voltage, the junction behaves like a capacitor. This is the depletion or junction capacitance. As reverse bias increases, the depletion width increases, so the capacitance decreases, just like a parallel plate capacitor with larger plate separation. This property is used in varactor diodes for tuning circuits. In the next class we will derive the expression for depletion width and capacitance as a function of doping and applied voltage.
